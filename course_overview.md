# DESG 260: Interface & Usability (User Experience 1)

## Course Purpose
Students will help encourage human flourishing and charitable human interactions through mastering the process of creating user experience design and crafting engaging user interfaces.

## Course Description

In this course, students will explore the process for developing digital products that serve users' needs. Students will prototype screen-based experiences that are empathetic to the needs of the end user. Students will develop design concepts that mediate relationships between people and products, environments, and services.

This is a project-based graphic design studio course with a balanced mix of technical instruction and critique. In this class you will think broadly about designing for different media and how designs change in response to time, space, interactions. In sum, you will learn to design experiences, not just visuals.

## Technical Skills & Concepts

You will learn to improve and assist your design skills using industry-standard software tools. You can certainly be a good designer and not know how to use design software, but you will be empowered to do more in your career and be more independent if you know the tools.

Although part of the class will teach technical skills, the goal is to create designed products and visuals with strong concepts and ideas, rooted in excellent typography. You can manipulate photos, or vectors, or illustrate like a pro, but you will never be a good designer until you learn how to use type, work conceptually, and think systematically.

## Learning Outcomes

### Better Interactions & Experiences
Students will be able to discuss critically and effectively design products and experiences that encourage human flourishing and charitable interactions.

### Critical Analysis of Form and Format
Students will be able to identify and critically discuss the affordances and constraints of different forms and formats of interactive media, demonstrating how they affect understanding and demand attention in all contexts, from consumer applications as well as faith and devotion.

### Problem Discovery & Research (Curiosity, Empathy, Humility)
Students will demonstrate discovery of design problems (challenges) to be solved through developing attributes of curiosity, empathy, and humility. They will demonstrate their learning through appropriate use of research methods, including qualitative and quantitative data collection, ethnography, empathy-building, and usability testing.

### Familiarity and Distinctiveness
Students will be able to create designs that appropriately balance familiar patterns and distinctive elements, creating products that show an understanding of long-standing mental models, platform-specific defaults and standards, as well as unique branding, typography, and interface styles within current design sensibilities.

### Design Strategy
Students will contextualize their visual design solutions into strategic frameworks, packaging design decisions within an understanding of the competitive landscape and creation of a distinctive brand.

### Communication and Feedback
Students will be able to defend and explain their design decisions, communicate clearly, and give and receive constructive feedback through a variety of reviews including usability testing, team and peer critique, and design reviews with faculty.

### Detailed Documentation
Students will be able to create detailed documentation of their design process and final design outcome, with clear written explanations of interface content and functionality.

## Backward Design: Stage 1 Elements

### Essential Questions (Course-Level)

1. **What is design?**

2. **What is the difference between forming intent and rendering intent—and why does good design require excellence in both?**

3. **What makes a design good or bad?** How do we move beyond personal preference to evaluate design decisions?

4. **How do designers balance familiarity and innovation?** When should we follow conventions, and when should we break them?

5. **What is the relationship between form and function in digital products?** Can something be beautiful and usable at the same time?

6. **How does empathy shape the design process?** What does it mean to truly design for someone else's needs rather than our own assumptions?

7. **How do constraints enable creativity?** Why do limitations (device size, platform guidelines, accessibility requirements) often lead to better solutions?

8. **What story does a design tell?** How do we communicate complex ideas, guide behavior, and create meaning through interfaces?

### Enduring Understandings

Students will understand that...

1. **Design is the forming and rendering of intent.** Forming intent means discovering the right problem and envisioning the right solution. Rendering intent means executing that vision with skill and craft.

2. **Good design requires excellence in both forming and rendering.** A brilliant idea poorly executed fails; a beautifully crafted solution to the wrong problem also fails. Bad design results from failure in either phase.

3. **Empathy, curiosity, and humility are essential for forming intent.** These qualities help designers understand problems from perspectives beyond their own and resist the temptation to design for themselves.

4. **Good design is often invisible.** Users notice friction, confusion, and frustration—not seamless experiences. The goal is to remove barriers, not to be noticed.

5. **Research and testing are design activities, not separate phases.** Understanding users, testing assumptions, and iterating based on feedback are central to the design process, not add-ons.

6. **Users bring mental models shaped by prior experience.** Effective interfaces leverage existing expectations while introducing new patterns thoughtfully.

7. **Design serves human flourishing.** Beyond usability and aesthetics, designers have a responsibility to consider the broader impact of their work on people's lives, relationships, and well-being.

8. **The design process is iterative and non-linear.** Discovery, definition, development, and delivery overlap and repeat. Embracing uncertainty and revision leads to stronger outcomes.

### Students Will Know

- The distinction between forming intent (discovering the right problem and envisioning the right solution) and rendering intent (executing that vision skillfully)
- How empathy, curiosity, and humility support forming intent
- How craft skills like typography, visual hierarchy, and prototyping support rendering intent
- The vocabulary and concepts of user experience design (affordances, signifiers, feedback, mental models, user flows, personas, wireframes, prototypes)
- Platform-specific design conventions (iOS Human Interface Guidelines, Material Design)
- Principles of visual hierarchy and typography in interface design
- Research methods for understanding users (interviews, observation, usability testing, competitive analysis)
- The stages of the design process (discovery, definition, development, delivery)
- How responsive design adapts to different screen sizes and contexts
- The relationship between branding, typography, and interface design
- Basic accessibility principles and why they matter

### Students Will Be Able To

**Forming Intent:**
- Conduct user research using interviews, observation, and competitive analysis
- Identify design problems worth solving through curiosity and empathy
- Create user flows, journey maps, and personas based on research
- Generate and validate product ideas before committing to execution

**Rendering Intent:**
- Sketch and prototype ideas at varying levels of fidelity (paper, wireframe, high-fidelity)
- Design interfaces that balance familiar patterns with distinctive visual identity
- Apply typographic hierarchy and grid systems to digital layouts
- Build interactive prototypes in Figma

**Both Forming and Rendering:**
- Facilitate usability tests and synthesize findings (tests both the intent and its execution)
- Defend design decisions with clear rationale grounded in user needs and design principles
- Document their design process in a professional case study format

## Project-Specific Backward Design Elements

### Project A: Wayfinding Map + Icon Set

**Focus:** Journeys, Destinations, Touchpoints

**Essential Questions:**
1. How do symbols communicate meaning across language and culture?
2. What makes a wayfinding system intuitive—and what happens when it fails?
3. How do designers create visual consistency across a system of icons?

**Understandings:**
- **Even a focused project like icon design requires both forming intent and rendering intent.** Forming intent: What must this icon communicate? To whom? In what context? Rendering intent: How do I execute it with visual clarity and consistency?
- Icons are not just decoration; they are functional communication tools that must be instantly recognizable
- Effective wayfinding requires understanding how people navigate physical and mental spaces
- A cohesive visual system requires establishing and following consistent rules (stroke weight, grid, style)

**Students Will Know:**
- The difference between icons, symbols, and pictograms
- Principles of icon design (simplicity, recognizability, consistency)
- How to use vector tools in Illustrator and/or Figma
- What makes wayfinding systems succeed or fail

**Students Will Be Able To:**
- Sketch icon concepts rapidly
- Create vectorized icons with consistent visual style
- Design a simple wayfinding system
- Critique icons for both function (usability) and form (aesthetics)

**Targeted Learning Outcomes:** Critical Analysis of Form and Format, Communication and Feedback

---

### Project B: Make it Better App

**Focus:** Product Design as Discovery and Problem Solving; Conventions

**Essential Questions:**
1. How do we identify what's broken in an existing product—and how do we know our fix is actually better?
2. When should designers follow platform conventions, and when is it appropriate to deviate?
3. What can we learn about user needs by watching people struggle?

**Understandings:**
- **Improving an existing product requires both forming better intent (discovering what's actually broken and for whom) and rendering that intent (executing improvements with appropriate craft).**
- Improving an existing product requires understanding why it was designed that way in the first place
- Usability testing reveals problems that designers cannot see on their own
- Platform conventions (iOS, Android, Web) exist because users have learned to expect them; breaking conventions has real costs

**Students Will Know:**
- How to create user flows and task diagrams
- Platform-specific design patterns and guidelines
- Methods for conducting competitive analysis
- Basic usability testing protocols

**Students Will Be Able To:**
- Analyze an existing app's strengths and weaknesses
- Create paper prototypes for rapid iteration
- Conduct usability tests with real users
- Translate test findings into design improvements
- Build a functional prototype in Figma

**Targeted Learning Outcomes:** Problem Discovery & Research, Familiarity and Distinctiveness, Communication and Feedback

---

### Project C: Hierarchy/Responsive Web

**Focus:** Responsive, Online, and Mobile Design

**Essential Questions:**
1. How does the same content need to change when viewed on different devices?
2. What role does typography play in guiding users through information?
3. How do designers create structure and hierarchy without relying on decoration?

**Understandings:**
- **Responsive design challenges both forming intent (what information hierarchy serves users across contexts?) and rendering intent (how do typography and layout execute that hierarchy?).**
- Content should drive design decisions; responsive design is about adapting presentation, not creating separate experiences
- Typography is the foundation of interface design—hierarchy, readability, and personality all flow from type choices
- Grids and systems create consistency and enable faster, more confident design decisions

**Students Will Know:**
- The box model and basic structure of web layouts
- How responsive grids adapt across breakpoints
- Principles of typographic hierarchy and scale
- The difference between system fonts and brand typography

**Students Will Be Able To:**
- Design layouts that adapt responsively to multiple screen sizes
- Establish and apply a typographic scale
- Use grids to create consistent, structured layouts
- Write basic HTML to understand how design translates to code
- Create style sheets that document design decisions

**Targeted Learning Outcomes:** Critical Analysis of Form and Format, Familiarity and Distinctiveness, Detailed Documentation

### Project D: Neue App (Capstone)

**Focus:** Problem Seeking, Product as Beautiful and Functional Interface

**Essential Questions:**
1. How do designers discover problems worth solving—rather than just solving problems handed to them?
2. What does it mean to design a product that is both beautiful and functional? Are these ever in tension?
3. How do research, strategy, and visual design come together in a cohesive product?
4. What story does your product tell about who it's for and what it values?

**Understandings:**
- **The capstone project requires the full cycle: forming intent through research and problem discovery, and rendering intent through visual identity, interaction design, and documentation.**
- The best design opportunities often come from observation and curiosity, not from briefs
- A product's visual identity (brand, typography, color, motion) is not separate from its usability—they work together to create experience
- Designing for real users requires ongoing research, testing, and humility about our assumptions
- Documentation and storytelling are essential design skills; a great product poorly communicated will not succeed

**Students Will Know:**
- Methods for idea generation and validation (interviews, competitive analysis, "Mom Test")
- How to scope a product to a realistic, achievable feature set
- The relationship between personas, user flows, and interface design
- How branding, typography, and iconography create product identity
- Principles of motion design and microinteractions
- Accessibility requirements and inclusive design principles

**Students Will Be Able To:**
- Generate and validate product ideas through research
- Create personas, journey maps, and user flows grounded in real data
- Design a complete product from paper prototype through high-fidelity mockup
- Develop a cohesive visual identity (brand, type, color, icons) for their product
- Build an interactive prototype with transitions and microinteractions
- Conduct multiple rounds of usability testing and iterate based on findings
- Document their entire process in a professional case study
- Present and defend their design decisions

**Targeted Learning Outcomes:** All seven learning outcomes, with emphasis on Better Interactions & Experiences, Problem Discovery & Research, Design Strategy, and Detailed Documentation
